Channel jamming mitigation explorer

An interactive companion to BOLT PR #1280, the local resource conservation proposal for mitigating channel jamming.

Parameters

Bucket liquidity split

% of liquidity; protected takes the remainder

Bucket slots

% of max_accepted_htlcs; protected takes the remainder

Per-peer general allocation

Channel statistics

The proposal splits every channel's incoming capacity into three buckets. General is open to anyone, no reputation needed — it is the bucket a stranger's payment lands in. Congestion is a smaller reserve that opens up when the channel is busy, and protected is held back for peers that have built a reputation for resolving HTLCs honestly. A channel has two things to divide this way: its max_accepted_htlcs slots, and its max_htlc_value_in_flight liquidity.

This table works out where those divisions land for channels of different sizes. One column per channel size, so you can see which numbers scale with the channel and which do not. The last three rows are the ones that bite: they give the largest single payment each bucket will admit, as a percentage of the channel's in-flight limit. "Channels to saturate general" asks the attacker's question instead — how many channels someone must open to occupy every general slot at once, given that each peer is assigned its slots at random.

Liquidity values are a percentage of max_htlc_value_in_flight. Saturation is a coupon-collector expectation over the random per-peer slot assignment.

Channel distribution

Knowing a bucket admits, say, 1.87% of a channel's liquidity does not tell you whether that is enough to be useful. This table answers the practical version: pick a dollar amount, and it reports the share of real mainnet channels that could carry a payment that size through the chosen bucket.

Each directed channel advertises a max_htlc, the largest single HTLC it will accept. The bucket lets a peer use some fraction of that, so the channel clears a $X payment when max_htlc × fraction ≥ $X. Every kept edge in the graph is tested against that bar and the table reports how many pass. Rows are the dollar thresholds, the outer columns are channel sizes, and the inner columns are BTC prices — a payment is a fixed number of dollars but a channel is a fixed number of sats, so the answer moves with the price. Darker cells mean more of the network clears.

Channel percentiles

The same relationship read the other way round. Rather than fixing a payment size and counting the channels that clear it, this fixes a position in the network and reports what a channel there can actually push through each bucket.

Rank every directed channel by its advertised max_htlc. The 50th percentile is the median channel — half the network advertises less, half more — and the 90th is a well-funded one. For each of those, the columns give the largest single payment the bucket admits, in dollars: through a single general slot, through the whole allocation one peer gets, and through a congestion slot. It is the quickest way to see whether a typical channel can move a useful amount of money without reputation, or only pocket change.

General-bucket routability

The three tables above look at one channel at a time. A payment does not cross one channel — it crosses a route, and it fails if any hop turns it away. This section follows that through, and it is where the proposal's limits start to bite in a way the per-channel view hides.

The question is narrow on purpose: how much payment traffic keeps working with no reputation at all? Reputation has to be earned, so a new node, a mobile wallet or anyone a routing node has not seen before is relying on the general bucket alone. If the general bucket cannot carry ordinary payments, those senders are pushed into building reputation before they can transact.

How the numbers are worked out

  1. One channel, one peer. The general bucket holds a share of the channel's liquidity spread over its slots, and one forwarding peer may occupy only k of those slots. That gives the largest single payment that peer can push through general: a fixed fraction of the channel's advertised max_htlc.
  2. One hop. Apply that fraction to every directed channel in the graph and count the ones still large enough to carry the payment. That share is the per-hop curve — the chance a single randomly chosen hop lets the payment through.
  3. A whole route. A route clears only if every hop on it clears. Treating hops as independent draws, a route of h hops clears with probability (per-hop)h. The main chart draws three hops, roughly the average Lightning path; the heatmap sweeps one through six.

Two curves, then, and the shaded wedge between them is what composing hops costs you. Because the per-hop share is a probability below one, raising it to a power drives it down fast: a hop that clears 80% of the time gives a three-hop route that clears about half the time. That gap is the whole reason to look at routes rather than channels.

The weighting toggle changes what is being counted, and the two answers differ enormously. By count gives every directed channel one vote, which answers "what share of channels can do this". By liquidity weights each channel by the size it advertises, which leans towards the well-funded channels a payment is more likely to be routed over. Neither is the share of real payments that succeed — that would need traffic data nobody publishes — so the honest reading is that the truth sits somewhere between the two, and the gap between them is a measure of how much the answer depends on where in the network you look.

What this does not account for

  • Hops are not really independent. A payment is routed deliberately, and pathfinding steers it towards well-funded channels rather than sampling the graph at random. Raising the per-hop share to a power therefore understates the truth, so read the route curve as a lower bound rather than an estimate. Replacing it with sampled real shortest paths would tighten the number without changing the shape.
  • Two of the three inputs are assumptions. Gossip publishes neither max_htlc_value_in_flight nor max_accepted_htlcs, so this page stands in each direction's advertised max_htlc for the first — the observable lower bound on it — and takes the slot count from the parameters above. Every figure here moves with those choices, which is why they are controls rather than constants.
  • The graph is a snapshot of what nodes advertise, not of what they will actually do. A channel can advertise a large max_htlc and still have no liquidity on the side you need.
