General-bucket routability

This section attempts to provide a visual representation of whether payments will fit into the general bucket, across different route lengths. It uses the following methodology:

  • Sort every node by the role it plays in routing: terminal never forwards for anyone, core is the smallest set carrying 90% of all transit, and peripheral is the rest. Degree would be the obvious axis and is the wrong one — the roles overlap heavily in channel count.
  • Sample routes between every ordered pair of roles, so each cell of the matrix gets its own quota rather than core-to-core vanishing into a rounding error.
  • Drop each route's first channel: the sender is not forwarding, so no general bucket applies to it.
  • Reduce the rest to the route's bottleneck — the smallest max_htlc among them.
  • Take the portion of routes whose bottleneck accommodates the payment in its general bucket.

Pick a cell to see who is paying whom. The receiver's role matters far more than the sender's, because the sender's own first channel is never gated — your role only shapes the route, while theirs sets the last gated channel. Cells are sampled separately and so are comparable with each other, but there is no meaningful overall figure to read off the grid.

A hop here is a node that forwards, so A→B→C is one hop and a direct payment is none. Because each hop count is a different set of node pairs, the heatmap rows are not nested — short routes are not the best case. A one-hop route exists precisely because both ends hang off the same large hub, and a hub with hundreds of channels holds small ones to each of its leaves, so its single gated channel is a bad one. The interior hub-to-hub channels are generous; it is the last mile that binds.

The same measurement over each hop count's own sample of routes, from one to six. Darker means more of the flow stays in general; the outlined column is the selected payment size, and dragging across the squares moves it.

What this does not account for

  • One route per pair. We take the shortest route, cheapest among equals, and never retry — a real sender has several routes to try, so this is a lower bound.
  • Unknown max_htlc_value_in_flight. This value is not gossiped. We use max_htlc_msat as a proxy, and where a direction advertises none we impute 99% of capacity.
  • One HTLC in flight. We assume a single HTLC occupying the outgoing channel's allocation on its incoming channel, and do not account for liquidity distribution.
