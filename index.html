Channel jamming mitigation explorer

An interactive companion to BOLT PR #1280, the local resource conservation proposal for mitigating channel jamming.

Parameters

Bucket liquidity split

% of liquidity; protected takes the remainder

Bucket slots

% of max_accepted_htlcs; protected takes the remainder

Per-peer general allocation

k = max(min slots, ⌊general slots × pct⌋)

Channel statistics

No graph data here — this is the parameters above applied to one channel of each size, so you can see which quantities scale with max_accepted_htlcs and which are pinned by the split.

Slot rows. In percentage mode the general and congestion counts are floored from their share of max_accepted_htlcs and protected takes the remainder, so all three scale together. In fixed mode the first two are constants and only protected varies. k is the per-peer allocation, max(min slots, ⌊general slots × pct⌋), capped at the general count.

Liquidity rows. Percentages of max_htlc_value_in_flight, not of the channel's total capacity. One general slot is the bucket's share divided by its slot count; the largest HTLC per outgoing channel is that figure times k, since a peer may fill its whole allocation with one HTLC; the congestion figure is one slot's worth.

Saturation. The expected number of channels an attacker opens before every general slot is occupied, when each is assigned k distinct slots uniformly at random. That is a coupon-collector problem with group draws; exact inclusion–exclusion is numerically unstable at these sizes, so it is estimated by 3,000 seeded Monte-Carlo trials and is deterministic across renders.

Channel distribution

The first table that touches the graph: each cell is the share of real mainnet channels whose bucket limit clears a payment of at least that size.

The data. An lncli describegraph dump, taken per direction rather than per channel. A direction is kept when the advertising node has more than one channel — single-channel nodes are assumed not to forward — and when it advertises a non-zero max_htlc_msat. That value, floored to sats, is the edge's base: it is the observable lower bound on max_htlc_value_in_flight, which gossip does not carry. The footer gives the kept and dropped counts.

The calculation. A threshold in dollars converts to sats at the column's BTC price, then divides by the bucket's fraction to give the base value an edge needs: required = usd → sat ÷ fraction. The kept values are held as an ascending histogram with a suffix-count array, so the share of edges at or above required is one binary search per cell.

Reading it. Rows are thresholds, outer columns are channel sizes, inner columns are BTC prices — a payment is a fixed number of dollars while a channel is a fixed number of sats, so the answer moves with price. Darker means a larger share of the network clears. The tab switches which bucket fraction is applied: the whole per-peer allocation, or one congestion slot.

Channel percentiles

The same edge set as above, inverted: instead of fixing a payment size and counting the edges that clear it, this fixes a position in the distribution and reports the dollars that position can move.

The calculation. Rank the kept directions by advertised max_htlc and take a nearest-rank percentile — the smallest observed value at or below which at least p% of edges fall, read off the same suffix array by binary search. That gives a sat figure per row.

The columns multiply that figure by each bucket fraction and convert to dollars at the selected price. Note that "all general slots" means all k slots one peer is allocated, not the whole general bucket — it is the largest single HTLC that peer can send, which is the quantity that binds in practice.

Caveats that carry over. These are percentiles of what nodes advertise, not of what they can settle, and the base value is a lower bound on the in-flight limit rather than the limit itself. Both push the real figures up, not down.

General-bucket routability

Everything above is per channel. This composes the per-hop figure along a route, which is where the general bucket's limits bind: clearance multiplies, so a route is far worse than its hops suggest.

How the curves are computed

  1. 1 Per-channel limit The largest single HTLC one peer can push through general is its k-slot allocation's worth of the bucket's liquidity — a fixed fraction of the edge's advertised max_htlc, the same fraction the tables above use.
  2. 2 Per hop Invert it: a payment needs an edge of at least payment ÷ fraction. The per-hop curve is the share of the kept edge set at or above that, swept across the payment axis — one binary search per sample, 160 samples per curve.
  3. 3 Per route A route clears only if every hop clears, so with hops treated as independent draws an h-hop route clears with probability (per-hop)h. The chart draws h = 3; the heatmap sweeps h = 1…6.

The two weightings. By count is qualifying edges ÷ all edges. By liquidity is Σ max_htlc of qualifying edges ÷ Σ max_htlc of all edges, computed from a parallel suffix-sum over the same histogram, so large edges count for more. Neither is the share of real payments that clear — that needs traffic data nobody publishes — and the two diverge by an order of magnitude at ordinary payment sizes, which is the honest measure of how much the answer depends on where you look.

The chart. Log payment axis over five decades. The shaded wedge is the difference between the two curves, i.e. what composing three hops costs: since the per-hop share is below one, raising it to a power falls away fast, and a hop clearing 80% of the time leaves a three-hop route at about half. The vertical line is the selected payment size and can be dragged.

The heatmap. The same per-hop curve raised to each power from one to six. Columns are 25 equal bins across the log axis, each taking the payment size at its midpoint; shading is five bands of a single-hue ramp, so a step in colour is a 20-point step in clearance. Dragging across it moves the same cursor.

The same calculation at every hop count from one to six, so you can see how quickly route length erodes what the general bucket can carry. Darker means more of the flow stays in general. The outlined column is the selected payment size — drag across the squares to move it.

What this does not account for

  • Hops are not really independent. A payment is routed deliberately, and pathfinding steers it towards well-funded channels rather than sampling the graph at random. Raising the per-hop share to a power therefore understates the truth, so read the route curve as a lower bound rather than an estimate. Replacing it with sampled real shortest paths would tighten the number without changing the shape.
  • Two of the three inputs are assumptions. Gossip publishes neither max_htlc_value_in_flight nor max_accepted_htlcs, so this page stands in each direction's advertised max_htlc for the first — the observable lower bound on it — and takes the slot count from the parameters above. Every figure here moves with those choices, which is why they are controls rather than constants.
  • The graph is a snapshot of what nodes advertise, not of what they will actually do. A channel can advertise a large max_htlc and still have no liquidity on the side you need.
